Roadmap

The whole reading in one paragraph

Value at risk is the minimum loss you should expect with a given probability over a given time frame (e.g. a 5% daily VaR of $2.2 million). It is estimated with the parametric method (normal, μ − zσ with z = 1.65 for 5%), historical simulation (rank actual past outcomes of today’s portfolio) or Monte Carlo (rank thousands of simulated outcomes). VaR is simple, comparable, accepted by regulators and backtestable, but subjective, blind to fat tails, liquidity and correlation spikes, and silent about the right tail. Its extensions are CVaR (average loss beyond VaR), incremental / marginal VaR (VaR change from a position change) and relative VaR (tracking error). Because VaR is not enough, managers add sensitivity measures (beta, duration/convexity, delta/gamma/vega — small changes, one factor, no probability) and scenario / stress measures (large changes, many factors, no probability). Each type of institution leans on a different toolkit, and firms turn these measures into constraints (risk budgets, position, scenario and stop-loss limits) and into capital allocation decisions.

LOS 38.a

Value at Risk (VaR) and Portfolio Risk

Explain the use of value at risk (VaR) in measuring portfolio risk.

What VaR measures

Value at risk (VaR) measures the downside risk of a portfolio. Formally (slide wording), it is a minimum loss to which a portfolio might be subject over a particular time period with a certain degree of probability. It has three components: the loss size, the probability of a loss greater than or equal to that size, and a time frame.

The three components of VaR Study aid
ComponentWhat it isTypical values
Minimum loss amountExpressed in currency units (USD, EUR…) or in percentage termse.g. $25,000 or 3% of portfolio value
Time frameThe period over which the firm is subject to the minimum lossa day, a week, a month, or any other relevant period
Degree of probabilityThe probability that the loss will equal or exceed the minimum amount; may also be stated as a confidence level1%, 5% and 16% probability ↔ 99%, 95% and 84% confidence

Reading a VaR statement — three equivalent ways

Take the statement: “There is a 5% probability that the company will experience a loss of $25,000 or more in any given month.” This is the same as stating that the monthly 5% VaR is $25,000: probability = 5%, loss size = $25,000, time frame = one month.

  • Minimum-loss form. $25,000 is a minimum loss amount, so: “5% of the time the minimum monthly loss that the company will experience is $25,000.”
  • Percentage form. VaR can be expressed in percentage terms: if the 5% monthly VaR is 3%, then 5% of the time the monthly portfolio value will fall by at least 3%.
  • Confidence-level form. We are 95% (= 100% − 5%) confident that the portfolio will experience a loss of no more than 3%.
Linking the forms
% VaR = $ VaR ÷ portfolio value   ·   confidence level = 100% − probability
Example = $2.2 million ÷ $44 million = 5% VaR as a percentage; 100% − 5% = 95% confidence

Choosing the inputs — judgment is involved

To estimate a VaR we must specify the time period and either the loss size or the probability, so there is significant judgment involved. If we choose the size of the loss, we estimate the probability of losses of that size or larger; if we choose the probability, we estimate the minimum size of the losses that will occur with that probability.

While any probability can be specified, VaR is typically expressed for 1%, 5% or 16% (one standard deviation below the mean for a normal distribution). The time frame also varies: a day, a week, a month, or any other relevant period.

Slide example — the $44 million portfolio

Consider a $44 million portfolio whose daily 5% VaR is $2.2 million. Here daily = time frame, 5% = probability, $2.2 million = minimum loss. Three equivalent readings:

  • The minimum loss we would expect to occur over one day 5% of the time is $2.2 million.
  • 5% of the time the daily portfolio value will fall by at least 5% ($2.2M ÷ $44M).
  • We would expect a daily loss of no more than $2.2 million 95% of the time.
Slide figure — histogram of daily returns with the 5% tail (illustrative) Study aid

Orange bars are the worst 5% of days; the first bar to the right of them marks the VaR cut-off.

Common traps — what VaR is not
  • VaR is not the maximum loss: it is a minimum loss for the tail. Losses beyond VaR are possible and VaR says nothing about how large they can be (see CVaR in LOS 38.e).
  • VaR is not the expected loss.
  • A 5% VaR does not mean “a 5% chance of losing exactly X”; it means a 5% chance of a loss of X or more.

Parametric, Historical Simulation and Monte Carlo

Compare the parametric (variance–covariance), historical simulation, and Monte Carlo simulation methods for estimating VaR.

Step zero — identify the risk factors

The first step in estimating the VaR for a portfolio is to identify the risk factors that enter into the determination of portfolio returns. These might include market risk, interest rate risk or currency risk, among others. Three methods then turn those risk factors into a distribution of portfolio outcomes.

The three methods side by side Study aid

1 · Parametric (variance–covariance) method

Generates a VaR estimate from return and standard deviation, typically from a normal distribution. Assuming normality lets us estimate portfolio risk from only the means, variances and covariances (or correlations) of the risk factors. A non-normal assumption would add complexity and require parameters such as skewness and kurtosis.

  • Advantages: conceptually simple; relatively easy to apply under normality.
  • Disadvantages: relies on simplifying assumptions such as normality of returns; estimates are only as good as the forecasts of means and standard deviations; very sensitive to the covariance estimate and to the lookback period; difficult to use when the portfolio has options (limited usefulness when normality cannot be assumed).

2 · Historical simulation method

Uses the current portfolio and reprices it using the actual historical changes in the key factors during the lookback period. Rank the outcomes; the VaR is read off the chosen percentile. No adjustments are made for differences between the lookback-period results and a longer prior period.

  • Advantages: conceptually simple; does not rely on assumptions about the return distribution (no normality needed); can be used for portfolios containing options because it relies on actual data (historical results include the changes in option values).
  • Disadvantages: meaningful only if the lookback-period returns are representative of future returns (“the past may not repeat itself”); a very volatile lookback period gives overestimates, a calm one likely underestimates.

3 · Monte Carlo simulation method

Based on an assumed probability distribution for each risk factor plus an assumption about the correlations between risk factors. Computer software generates random values for each factor; pricing models convert each set of factor changes into a change in portfolio value. Repeated thousands of times, the outcomes are ordered and the fifth percentile (5% of outcomes lower) gives the 5% VaR.

  • Advantages: any return distribution can be assumed; works for portfolios that contain options.
  • Disadvantages: output is only as good as the input assumptions; the software needs a lot of computing power and complexity.
Monte Carlo = parametric (when…)

Assuming a large sample size, the Monte Carlo method produces identical results to the parametric method if the distribution specified and the parameters are the same (Example 30 Q4).

Lookback period

The returns period used to estimate the mean and standard deviation of each risk factor is the lookback period. For the variance of daily returns we might use the last two years; for the variance of annual returns we would choose a longer lookback. The important point: the parameter estimates should be those we expect over the period for which we are estimating the VaR; estimates based on recent periods may be adjusted toward longer-term averages.

Lookback period (redrawn) Study aid

Which methods use a lookback period? The parametric method and historical simulation both use one. Monte Carlo uses the estimated statistical properties of each risk factor, so a lookback period is least likely to be specified there (Module Quiz 38.1 Q2).

Key Concepts — LOS 38.b

Parametric method — uses the estimated variances and covariances of portfolio securities to estimate the distribution of possible portfolio values, often assuming a normal distribution.

Historical simulation — uses historical values for risk factors over some prior lookback period to get a distribution of possible values.

Sign convention

Interpreting a VaR estimate

The x% VaR is the minimum loss for the current portfolio, x% of the time, based on an estimated distribution of portfolio values. E.g. a 5% daily VaR of $2,167,500 means: on 5% of trading days we expect a loss of at least $2,167,500; on the other 95% of days the loss will be smaller (or there is a gain).

LOS 38.d

Advantages and Limitations of VaR

Describe advantages and limitations of VaR.

VaR, as a measure of portfolio risk, has many benefits but suffers from the same limitations as many other forward-looking estimates of portfolio risk.

Advantages of VaR

  • Simple concept — easy to explain, although the details of the methodology can be complex; it expresses risk as a single number.
  • Basis for risk comparison — lets the risk of different portfolios, asset classes or trading operations be compared to gain a sense of relative riskiness.
  • Performance evaluation (returns generated vs. risk taken) — instead of judging a trading group on returns alone, compute the ratio of trading income to VaR (profit per dollar of VaR).
  • Capital allocation / risk budgeting — when allocating capital to trading units, risk managers can look at the allocation of VaR and optimize the allocation of capital given the firm’s maximum VaR. Example: the equity trading desk may be assigned a maximum daily VaR of $5 million while the more profitable currency desk gets $15 million.
  • Regulatory acceptance — global banking regulators accept VaR as a measure of financial risk, although they do not prescribe estimation methods or impose a maximum VaR.
  • Reliability can be verified by backtesting.

Limitations of VaR

  • Subjectivity / many choices. VaR needs a loss percentage (probability), a lookback period, distribution assumptions and parameter estimates, and is significantly affected by each. An unscrupulous analyst can choose assumptions that lead to a low VaR. (Key Concepts: “vulnerable to misspecification by the user”.)
  • Underestimates tail risk when normality is assumed. Actual return distributions frequently have “fatter tails” than the normal, so normal-based VaRs underestimate the probability of extreme outcomes. Normality is not required by VaR but is almost always used, especially with the parametric method.
  • Ignores liquidity. Liquidity often falls significantly when asset prices fall; a VaR that does not account for this understates the actual losses when liquidating positions under extreme price pressure.
  • Correlation risk. Correlations spike during financial stress. VaR based on normal correlation levels overestimates diversification benefits and underestimates potential losses.
  • Oversimplification / misunderstanding. As a single summary number, many aspects of risk are not quantified; users must understand the limitations to use VaR appropriately (slide: “misunderstanding the meaning of VaR”, “oversimplification”).
  • Left-tail only. VaR focuses only on downside risk and extreme negative outcomes; including the right-hand tail gives a better understanding of the risk–return trade-off (slide: “disregard of right-tail events”). It also says nothing about how far losses can go beyond the VaR.
  • Vulnerable to trending or volatility regimes (slide): parameters estimated in one regime may not hold when conditions change.

Why a normal VaR understates tail risk (illustrative) Study aid

Both curves have the same mean and standard deviation. The fat-tailed curve (a Student-t, 4 d.f., rescaled — an illustration) puts more probability far in the left tail than the normal curve used by the parametric method: for example, about 1.5% of its outcomes fall below the normal curve’s 1% VaR cut-off.

🧮 Correlation-spike lab — diversification evaporates in a crisis Study aid
Key idea

In normal times a low correlation gives a small portfolio σ and a small VaR. If correlations spike toward +1 in a crisis, the actual portfolio σ — and the true VaR — is much larger than the VaR computed with the normal-time correlation. That is why VaR “overestimates diversification benefits”.

Extensions of VaR

Describe extensions of VaR.

Conditional VaR (CVaR)

CVaR is the expected loss, given that the loss is equal to or greater than the VaR; hence its other names, expected tail loss and expected shortfall. It answers the question VaR cannot: “if we are in the worst 5%, how bad is it on average?” When VaR comes from historical simulation or Monte Carlo we have all the losses greater than the VaR loss, so CVaR is simply the average of those losses. With the parametric method we do not know the size of the losses beyond the VaR, so calculating the left-tail expected loss is mathematically complex.

CVaR on the distribution (slide figure, redrawn) Study aid

Slide numbers: 5% VaR = −$1.6 million; the expected value of the observations below VaR (CVaR) = −$2.7 million.

Incremental VaR (IVaR)

IVaR is the change in VaR from a change in the portfolio allocation to a security. Example: if increasing the weight of a security raises the portfolio VaR from $1,345,600 to $1,562,400, then IVaR = 1,562,400 − 1,345,600 = $216,800. Note Schweser words this as “a 2% increase in the weight”, the slide as “a 5% increase”; the dollar figures and the IVaR are the same.

Marginal VaR (MVaR)

MVaR is the slope of a curve that plots VaR as a function of a security’s weight in the portfolio, taken at the point corresponding to the security’s current weight. We interpret it as the change in VaR for a 1% increase in the weight. That is not precisely correct (a slope at a point is not the slope over a 1% change) but is a reasonable approximation. Both MVaR and IVaR estimate the change in VaR from changing the weight of a single security: IVaR for a specified (finite) change, MVaR for a small change.

Relative VaR = ex ante tracking error

Relative VaR measures the VaR of the difference between the portfolio return and the return on its benchmark. A 5% monthly relative VaR of 2.5% implies that 5% of the time the portfolio’s relative underperformance will be at least 2.5%. It can be computed as the VaR of a combination of a long position in the subject portfolio and a short position in the benchmark portfolio.

Sensitivity and Scenario Measures vs VaR

Describe sensitivity risk measures and scenario risk measures and compare these measures to VaR.

Given the limitations of VaR as a risk measure, analysts should use other risk measures that complement VaR: sensitivity measures and scenario measures.

Sensitivity vs scenario (redrawn) Study aid

Sensitivity analysis

Focuses on the effect on portfolio value of a small change in one risk factor. By examining the sensitivity of portfolio value to several risk factors, portfolio risk can be better understood and more effectively managed. It is used to measure equity exposure, fixed-income exposure and options exposure (LOS 38.g). Sensitivity analysis complements VaR but, unlike VaR, does not involve any prediction of the probability of losses of any specific amount.

Scenario analysis

Estimates the effect on portfolio value of a set of changes of significant magnitude in multiple risk factors. The scenarios used are often changes expected to cause a significant decline in portfolio value, though a scenario that increases value may also be considered.

Equity, Fixed-Income and Options Exposure Measures

Demonstrate how equity, fixed-income, and options exposure measures may be used in measuring and managing market risk and volatility risk.

The risk factors used to measure the risks of equities, fixed-income securities and options are all different. Together they let a manager measure and manage market risk and volatility risk by adjusting portfolio holdings to control exposure to each factor.

Exposure measures at a glance Study aid

1 · Equity exposure — beta

For equities the most often used risk factor is beta: how the returns of a security or portfolio are expected to be affected by overall market returns. The CAPM is based on market risk as measured by beta and concludes that the expected return on an asset equals the risk-free rate plus beta times the market risk premium:

CAPM
E(Ri) = RF + βi [ E(RM) − RF ]
βi = sensitivity of asset i to market returns
E(RM) − RF = market risk premium
Beta as the equity risk factor (illustrative) Study aid

2 · Fixed-income exposure — duration and convexity

Duration estimates how market values are affected by changes in interest rates (yields to maturity) — the first-order effect. For larger rate changes, convexity (the sensitivity of a bond’s duration to changes in rates — the second-order effect) improves the estimate. Together they estimate the percentage change in value of a fixed-income security or portfolio for a change in YTM (ΔY):

Duration + convexity
Change in price (%) ≈ − Duration × (ΔY) + ½ × Convexity × (ΔY)2
ΔY = change in yield to maturity (as a decimal, e.g. +1% = 0.01)
Professor’s Note

If the duration in the previous equation is Macaulay duration (rather than modified duration), ΔY is replaced by ΔY / (1 + Y).

Duration is the slope, convexity the curvature Study aid

The straight tangent line (duration only) understates the price after a yield fall and overstates the fall after a yield rise; the convexity term closes the gap.

Duration and delta measure first-order effects of risk-factor changes; convexity and gamma are second-order effects. Equity risk is measured by beta (sensitivity to overall market returns).

The interest rate risk of fixed-income securities is measured by duration (sensitivity to change in yield) and convexity (second-order effect, change in duration).

Options risk is measured by delta (sensitivity to asset price changes), gamma (second-order effect, change in delta) and vega (sensitivity to asset price volatility).

Market risk can be managed by adjusting portfolio holdings to control the exposures to these various risk factors.

Module 38.3 · Sensitivity and Scenario Risk Measures
LOS 38.h

Use of Sensitivity and Scenario Risk Measures

Describe the use of sensitivity risk measures and scenario risk measures.

Why use sensitivity measures?

Sensitivity risk measures inform a portfolio manager about the portfolio’s exposure to various risk factors to facilitate risk management. Exposure to risks the manager believes are excessive can be reduced (hedged). Eliminating all risk is not the goal: portfolios that are not exposed to risk can be expected to earn only the risk-free rate of return. Sensitivity analysis can give a risk manager a more complete view of the vulnerability of a portfolio to a variety of risk factors.

Scenario measures with options and embedded options

When scenario analysis is applied to a portfolio that contains options or fixed-income securities with embedded options, the individual options and bonds must be valued with a pricing model using the scenario values of the risk factors. Factor sensitivities (delta, duration…) estimate the effect of small changes; for larger changes, pricing models must be used. Even combining first- and second-order effects (duration + convexity) only approximates the value change from a relatively large move.

Pricing models can be quite accurate when all relevant characteristics of a security are specified. In scenario analysis, each portfolio security is model-priced using the risk-factor values of the scenario to estimate the impact on portfolio value. A scenario risk measure estimates the portfolio return that would result from either a hypothetical market event or the recurrence of an actual historical event.

Instantaneous vs incremental scenarios

Scenario analysis is often performed as if the scenario changes were instantaneous. In some cases the changes are modeled as incremental (for example daily), and the scenario includes portfolio-manager actions in response to each change — reducing or closing positions, adjusting hedges. Because such actions reduce the overall impact, an instantaneous scenario is considered more conservative. It is also more realistic when counterparties are unable or unwilling to provide additional collateral that is required, or when lack of liquidity makes changing positions very costly or impossible.

Instantaneous vs incremental (redrawn) Study aid

Reverse stress testing

In a reverse stress test the first step is to identify the portfolio’s largest risk exposures. Then an unacceptable outcome is determined (usually one that would threaten the survival of the organization), and the scenarios of risk-factor changes that would produce that outcome are identified. The question then becomes how likely such scenarios are. Used this way, scenario analysis helps risk managers identify vulnerabilities of a portfolio and perhaps mitigate the exposures found.

Where scenario analysis fits

Scenario analysis can be seen as the final step in the risk assessment and management process, after sensitivity analysis. For a firm that has limited its risk through a maximum VaR, limits on position sizes and limits on specific risk exposures, scenario analysis provides additional information on the portfolio’s vulnerability to a set of events, or to changes in correlations, that would significantly reduce portfolio value.

Firms that use leverage, especially banks and hedge funds, often use stress tests involving a single risk factor to find the size of change in that factor that could cause losses large enough to compromise the firm’s sustainability.

Key Concepts — LOS 38.h

A stress test based on either sensitivity or scenario analysis uses extreme changes to examine the expected effects on a portfolio or organization, often to determine the effects on a firm’s equity or solvency. A reverse stress test is designed to identify scenarios that would result in business failure.

Sensitivity analysis can give a risk manager a more complete view of the vulnerability of a portfolio to a variety of risk factors. Sensitivity and scenario risk measures provide additional information about portfolio risk but do not necessarily provide probabilities or, in the case of sensitivity measures, the sizes of expected changes in risk factors and portfolio value.

They provide information VaR does not and are not necessarily based on historical results. A historical scenario will not necessarily be repeated; hypothetical scenarios may be misspecified; the probability that a scenario will occur is unknown.

Advantages and Limitations of Sensitivity and Scenario Measures

Describe advantages and limitations of sensitivity risk measures and scenario risk measures.

VaR, sensitivity analysis and scenario analysis complement each other; a risk manager should not rely on only one of them.

Three measures, three different pieces of information Study aid
MeasureWhat it providesWhat it lacks
VaRA probability of lossOnly left-tail outcomes; depends on method and assumptions
Sensitivity analysisEstimates of the relative exposures to different risk factorsNo estimate of the probability of any specific change in risk factors (and no size of expected changes)
Scenario analysisInformation on exposure to simultaneous changes in several risk factors or changes in risk correlationsNo probability associated with a specific scenario (other than the empirical probability of a historical scenario over the lookback period)

Advantages and limitations (slide table)

✅
Advantages

  • Sensitivity measures address some of the shortcomings of position-size measures.
  • Scenario analysis does not need to rely on history.
  • Scenarios can be designed to overcome any assumption of normal distributions.
  • Scenarios can be tailored to expose a portfolio’s most concentrated positions to even worse movement than its other exposures.

⚠️
Limitations

  • Sensitivities typically do not distinguish assets by volatility.
  • Historical scenarios are not going to happen in exactly the same way again.
  • Hypothetical scenarios may incorrectly specify how assets will co-move.
  • Hypothetical scenarios can be very difficult to create and maintain.

The classic limitation — same duration, different yield volatility

Consider two bond portfolios with the same duration, so a one-basis-point change in yield changes both values by the same amount. The problem with duration as the risk measure is that the yield volatility of one portfolio can be quite different from the other’s. Government, investment-grade, corporate and high-yield bonds have quite different yield volatilities, so the probabilities of a given percentage decrease in value are quite different too. Likewise, option delta (or delta and gamma) may be appropriate for small price changes, but the volatility of the underlying may be very different across options.

🧮 Same duration, different yield volatility Study aid
Two more limitations (Example 32 Q5)

It is difficult to ascribe a probability to a given scenario, and scenario analysis risks being an infinite task — one cannot possibly measure all possible future scenarios. By contrast, “scenario measures assume a normal distribution” is not a limitation: scenarios can be designed to avoid that assumption.

Key Concepts — LOS 38.i

VaR, sensitivity analysis and scenario analysis complement each other, and a risk manager should not rely on only one of these measures.

VaR provides a probability of loss.

Sensitivity analysis provides estimates of the relative exposures to different risk factors, but does not provide estimates of the probability of any specific movement in risk factors.

Scenario analysis provides information about exposure to simultaneous changes in several risk factors or changes in risk correlations, but there is no probability associated with a specific scenario.

Banks

Banks typically use sensitivity measures (duration of held-to-maturity securities and foreign exchange risk exposure), scenario analysis and stress testing (for their full balance sheets — extreme, targeted shocks against all firm capital), leverage risk measures (e.g. the leverage ratio), and VaR (especially for trading securities). Banks also estimate liquidity gap (risk from asset–liability mismatches), estimate VaR for economic capital, and disaggregate risk by geographic location and by business unit type.

Professor’s Note

Economic capital is the amount of capital a firm needs to hold for it to survive severe losses due to the risks in its businesses.

Traditional (long-only) asset managers

Typically focus on relative risk measures unless their goal is an absolute-return target. Common measures: size of positions, sensitivity measures of interest-rate and market risk (beta for equities, various durations for bonds, delta for options), historical and hypothetical scenario analysis and stress tests, and options risk.

  • Active share — the difference between the weight of a security in the portfolio and its weight in the benchmark.
  • Ex-post tracking error (backward looking) — tracking error relative to a benchmark over a lookback period; used for performance attribution and to assess manager skill over prior periods.
  • Ex ante tracking error (forward looking) — used for risk estimation; focuses on the potential underperformance of the current (not a historical) portfolio.
  • Liquidity — daily average trading volume and days to liquidate; redemption risk — liquidity risk: the portion of the fund subject to redemption during peak times.
  • VaR — for managers with an absolute-return target.

Hedge funds

The measures depend to some extent on the strategy. In general: sensitivity analysis, leverage measures, liquidity, scenario analysis and stress tests. Funds with both long and short positions estimate measures for the long positions, the short positions and the overall portfolio (gross exposure = absolute value of long and short positions). Hedge funds that use VaR focus on VaR with high confidence levels (greater than 90%, i.e. probability below 10%) over short periods (weekly, daily). Funds with significantly non-normal returns use maximum drawdown — the largest decrease in value over prior periods of a specific length. Sensitivities based on standard deviation or beta may be misleading for large changes in risk factors when returns are non-normal.

Defined benefit pension funds

They compare the present value of assets (often market values) with the present value of estimated future liabilities (payments to retirees and heirs). Key measures:

  • Surplus at risk — a VaR for plan assets minus liabilities. A negative surplus must be made up by the firm unless higher-than-expected asset returns reduce it over time. Zero surplus at risk means precisely funding the liabilities with long fixed-income positions of comparable maturities.
  • Interest-rate / yield-curve sensitivity.
  • Glide path — a multi-year plan for adjusting pension contributions to reverse a significant overfunded or underfunded status.
  • Liability-hedging (hedged) vs return-generating (unhedged) exposures — splitting the portfolio into investments designed to match the liability and those meant to generate excess return. To reduce surplus uncertainty a fund may match assets to liabilities.

Insurance companies

Insurers are often subject to significant regulation of their products and investment portfolios (reserves).

Property & casualty (P&C) insurers sell auto, home, boat, liability and health insurance. Their insurance risks are not highly correlated with the market risk of their investment portfolios, and are reduced by reinsurance and geographic diversification. Premium income is expected to cover claims in a typical year; the investment portfolio is available for extraordinary claims (e.g. a natural-disaster year). They use sensitivities (managers set exposure limits and design an asset allocation within them), VaR and capital at risk / economic capital (additional capital is typically needed only when payouts exceed premium revenue; modeling must include payout risk as well as market risk), and scenario analysis that combines portfolio risk factors and insurance risk factors. Regulators may require specific reserves (based on policies issued) and discount the values of riskier assets held as reserves in determining their adequacy.

Life insurers sell life insurance policies and annuities (some pay until the annuity owner’s death). Their insurance risk is more highly correlated with the market risk of the investment portfolio. Annuities pay over long periods, so the present values of these liabilities are very sensitive to the discount factors used (plus mortality risk: the longer a life annuity pays, the larger the current liability). Hence life insurers estimate sensitivities to market risk factors for both the investment portfolio and the annuity liabilities, consider the risk of the remaining asset–liability mismatch (they can somewhat match assets to liabilities — more closely than P&C insurers), and use scenario analysis including non-market (insurance, e.g. longevity) and market risk factors.

Key Concepts — LOS 38.l

Banks are concerned with many risks including asset-liability mismatches, market risk for their investment portfolio, their leverage, the duration and convexity of their portfolio of fixed-income securities, and the overall risk to their economic capital.

Asset managers are most concerned with returns volatility and the probability distribution of either absolute losses or losses relative to a benchmark portfolio.

Pension fund managers are concerned with any mismatch between assets and liabilities as well as with the volatility of the surplus (assets minus liabilities).

LOS 38.j

Constraints: Risk Budgeting and Position, Scenario and Stop-Loss Limits

Explain constraints used in managing market risks, including risk budgeting, position limits, scenario limits, and stop-loss limits.

Constraints imposed to limit risk can be too restrictive (impairing profitability) or not restrictive enough (leading to financial stress, corporate reorganization or bankruptcy). Restrictions imposed at the business-unit level may be too restrictive to the extent that diversification benefits or offsetting positions across business units are not taken into account.

The four constraints (redrawn) Study aid

Risk budgeting

A risk-management process that first determines the acceptable total risk for an organization and then allocates that risk to different activities, strategies or asset classes as appropriate. Risk is generally measured by ex ante tracking error or VaR. Example: first set the maximum allowable 5% VaR, then allocate that VaR across business units. A portfolio manager may set a limit on total risk relative to a benchmark and then allocate it to deviations from the target asset allocation, deviations from benchmark weights in specific industries, and deviations from benchmark weights for firms within an industry.

Position limits

Limits on the market value of any given investment; they ensure a minimum level of diversification by limiting risk exposures. They may apply to allocations to individual securities within an asset class, asset classes such as equities or high-yield bonds, investment in a single country, securities in a single currency, or the difference between long and short positions for a hedge fund manager. They can be expressed as currency amounts or as percentages of portfolio value, and can also be based on a liquidity measure such as average daily or weekly trading volume.

Scenario limits

Limits on the expected loss for a given scenario: the portfolio must be adjusted so that the loss expected under that scenario does not exceed the specified amount.

Stop-loss limits

Require that a risk exposure be reduced (by sale or hedging) or closed out if losses exceed a specified amount over a certain period of time. A simple example: cut the allocation to a stock or asset class (by a given amount) if it declines by more than a specified percentage or currency amount. A slightly more complex type requires that an exposure be hedged as the value of a security or index falls; when a portfolio is hedged with index puts this is called portfolio insurance.

Risk Measures and Capital Allocation Decisions

Explain how risk measures may be used in capital allocation decisions.

Capital allocation decisions are about how the firm’s capital is used to fund its various business units or activities — analogous to asset allocation for a portfolio manager.

Ignoring risk, the optimal allocation would be the one that maximizes the expected return on the firm’s invested capital. Risk management, however, requires that the risk exposure of each use of capital be considered. One way to bring risk into the decision is to limit the overall risk of all activities: calculate a VaR for each activity or business unit, then allocate the maximum acceptable VaR across the units in a process similar to risk budgeting for a portfolio manager. This is only one method of considering risk exposures when allocating firm capital.
